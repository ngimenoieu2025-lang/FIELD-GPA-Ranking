We analysed three public datasets separately because their units of observation differ and they have no shared identifiers. The Spanish records let us compare fields within universities over time; the Portuguese records let us compare individual students and evaluate ranking rules against later performance; the US records provide an external comparison. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises their coverage and preparation. Its unit-of-observation row is essential: a Spanish row represents a group of graduates, a Portuguese row one student, and a US source row a course or section.

\subsection{Spanish Ministry Data}
\label{sec:data-spain}

We used the Sistema Integrado de Informaci\'on Universitaria (SIIU) table of graduates' final grades, combining both sexes (Ministerio de Ciencia, Innovaci\'on y Universidades, ``Media''). Each observation is the mean final grade of graduates in one field at one university in one academic year, on a 5--10 scale. For example, the Nursing observation for A Coru\~na in 2019--20 reports 7.85: this is an average over that field's graduates, not one student's mark. We also retained the published quartiles and maximum. The dataset contains 12,125 observations from 89 universities and 29 fields over 2015--16 to 2023--24. This is 52\% of the 23,229 possible combinations; suppression of small cells and differences in programme coverage leave an incomplete panel. We analysed reported cells without inventing missing grades.

We matched graduate counts by university, field and year to obtain selection weights and a measure of size (Ministerio de Ciencia, Innovaci\'on y Universidades, ``Egresados''). Valid quartiles and counts were available for 10,476 records. We also matched dropout rates for 86 universities and admission grades for 47 public universities. University admission grades are published for five broad branches rather than the 29 fields, while a separate table gives national admission grades by field (Ministerio de Ciencia, Innovaci\'on y Universidades, ``Nota media''). We standardised admission grades as z-scores before adjustment. These branch averages cannot distinguish intake between fields within the same branch, limiting our interpretation of the controls.

\subsection{Portuguese Student Records}
\label{sec:data-portugal}

We used the public, anonymised records of 4,424 students enrolled at one Portuguese polytechnic from 2008--09 to 2018--19 (Realinho et al., ``Predict Students''). Each row describes one student, including degree, entry grade, background and semester results. We calculated first-year grade point average (GPA) on the 0--20 scale by weighting the two semester averages by units passed. We excluded students who passed no unit because their average is undefined, rather than a genuine zero, and excluded degrees with fewer than 50 students. This left 3,703 students in 15 degrees; the ranking comparison used the 3,469 with grades in both semesters. We standardised entry grades within admission route because routes assess applicants differently. These individual records allow us to evaluate first-semester selection against second-semester performance and supply the background variables in Figure~\ref{fig:background}. We used the data under the repository's Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence without attempting to identify students.
